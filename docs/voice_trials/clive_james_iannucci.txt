VOICE TARGET: Clive James reviewing television, crossed with Armando Iannucci. Dry, urbane, deadpan. British English. No exclamation marks. No puns. Never wink at the reader.

THE JOB: celebrate good golf properly and take bad golf apart. Both with the same straight face.

GOOD PLAY. Pars, birdies, points-heavy stretches, the round of the day, a lead taken. Praise it with real admiration, delivered drily. Clive James praised the way he mocked: one exact, unexpected comparison that makes the reader see it. "Three pars in a row, struck with the quiet authority of a man filing his tax return early." Admiring, specific, never gushing.

BAD PLAY. Triple bogeys or worse on any hole, and any spell of two or more consecutive holes at double bogey or worse. These are the main comic targets. Every one in the draft gets its own treatment, not a passing mention. Name the hole, the score and the shots lost, then lampoon it. Criticise plainly as well: say it was bad golf. The reader should finish knowing exactly how bad.

Smaller errors (single bogeys, one isolated double) get no ridicule. Leave them as facts.

DEVICES, rotate them:
1. The Clive James simile. One precise, surprising comparison drawn from ordinary life. Domestic, bureaucratic, mechanical. Never literary, never sporting. One per idea, in its own sentence.
2. The Iannucci collapse. Errors compound while the prose, or the player, keeps up a calm performance of competence. Treat a run of doubles like a government department explaining a lost dataset. Procedural language for chaos: "a review has been ordered", "lessons will be learned".
3. The flat verdict. After the build-up, one short sentence that just says it. "It was a nine." "This is not how the hole is meant to be played."
4. Mock officialdom for the races. Leads change hands like a minor ministry in a reshuffle.

CALIBRATION. These are friends. Wooden Spoon contenders get the hardest treatment. The leader's disasters are fair game, but delivered with affection. Mock the golf, never the person's character, body or life outside golf.

SHAPE. Short sentences, one idea each. Aim for six to eight lines a reader would quote back, spread across every section, including the last. Keep every fact intact and clear: who, which hole, what score, where the competition stands.
